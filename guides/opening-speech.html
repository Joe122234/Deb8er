How to Write an Opening Speech for MUN

⚠️ MUN Pronoun Rule: In MUN, delegates represent countries — not themselves. Never use personal pronouns (I, we, us, me, my, our) in speeches, position papers, resolutions, or dialogue. Always use your country or delegation name instead. For example, say "The delegation of Japan supports…" rather than "I support…".

What is an Opening Speech?

An opening speech is your first impression in committee. It is the brief, 1–2 minute address each delegate delivers from their seat at the start of the conference, typically in the first formal session. This is not a casual introduction — it is a strategic statement that sets the tone for your entire participation in the committee.

The opening speech accomplishes three critical objectives. First, it signals to the chair and fellow delegates that you are prepared and knowledgeable about the topic. Second, it communicates your country's official position so other delegates know where you stand before caucusing begins. Third, it establishes your credibility and confidence as a speaker — qualities that directly influence how seriously other delegates take your proposals throughout the conference.

Judges and chairs use the opening speech to assess three things: preparation, confidence, and communication skills. A strong opening speech demonstrates that you have researched your country's policies, understand the global issue, and can present your position clearly under time pressure. It is the single highest-leverage moment in the early stages of committee — one minute that shapes how every other delegate perceives you for the rest of the conference.

Critically, the opening speech is not a position paper read aloud. It is not a persuasive essay or a personal opinion piece. It is a focused diplomatic statement delivered from the perspective of a sovereign nation, designed to be heard once and remembered. Every word must earn its place.

The 6-Part Structure

Most successful opening speeches follow a consistent six-part framework. This structure ensures you cover all the essential elements while staying within the 1–2 minute time limit. Think of it as a funnel: grab attention, state the problem, show your position, prove your credibility, propose solutions, and close with confidence.

Each section has a specific time allocation. The hook takes about 15 seconds and exists solely to make people listen. The issue section takes 20 seconds and gives just enough context. Country position takes 20 seconds to establish where you stand. Past actions take 15 seconds to prove your delegation acts, not just talks. The solution section is the longest at 30 seconds because it is your most important contribution. The closing takes 10 seconds and wraps up cleanly.

Writing Each Section

1
Hook (15 seconds)
Your hook is the single most important sentence in your entire speech. It is the first thing the chair, judges, and fellow delegates hear, and it determines whether they tune in or tune out. A strong hook grabs attention within the first 10 seconds and makes the committee want to hear what comes next. There are four reliable hook types that work consistently in MUN: rhetorical questions, startling statistics, quotes from UN officials, and personal connections.
💡 Start with a hook that makes people think. Rhetorical questions work because they force the audience to engage mentally. A statistic works because it shocks. A quote works because it carries authority. Choose the type that fits your personality and committee tone.

Rhetorical question: "What would you do if you had 10 years to save your country from disappearing beneath the ocean?"

Startling statistic: "Every 12 seconds, a child somewhere in the world dies from a vaccine-preventable disease — that is 7,000 children a day."

UN quote: "As the Secretary-General stated in his 2024 report, 'We are in a race against time — and time is losing.'"

Personal connection: "France has experienced three major floods in the past five years, displacing over 200,000 citizens."

2
Issue (20 seconds)
Briefly define the problem and its global significance in one to two sentences. Do not go deep into background here — your position paper is the place for that. The opening speech issue section exists only to make sure everyone in the room understands what you are talking about. Reference the UN's involvement or a relevant international framework to ground the topic in institutional context. Keep it tight.
⚠️ Do not over-explain the issue. Your audience already read the topic background. Spend no more than two sentences establishing context before moving to your country's position.

Sample sentence: "Artificial intelligence is transforming every sector of the global economy, yet international regulation has failed to keep pace, leaving billions of people unprotected."

3
Country Position (20 seconds)
State your delegation's official stance on the issue. This is where you demonstrate that you have researched your country's actual policies, not your personal beliefs. Reference specific commitments, treaties your country has signed, or policy frameworks your government has adopted. The chair is evaluating whether you understand your country's diplomacy — not whether you have strong personal opinions.
✦ Research your country's ACTUAL policy. If your country abstained on a key resolution, say so — that is more impressive than fabricating support. Specificity beats enthusiasm every time.

Sample sentence: "France firmly believes that AI governance requires a multilateral framework rooted in human rights, and has adopted its own national AI strategy aligned with the OECD AI Principles."

4
Past Actions (15 seconds)
Provide concrete evidence of your country's engagement with the issue. Reference specific UN resolutions your country co-sponsored, bilateral agreements, domestic legislation, or funding commitments. This paragraph proves your delegation does not just talk — it acts. Numbers, resolution numbers, and dollar amounts are your strongest tools here. Keep it to one or two specific examples.
💡 Reference specific UN resolutions by number. This shows the committee you have done your research. For example, citing "UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021)" is far stronger than saying "the UN has passed resolutions on this topic."

Sample sentence: "France co-sponsored the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence in 2021 and committed €100 million to AI research through its National AI Research Programme."

5
Solution (30 seconds)
This is the most important section of your speech — it gets the most time for a reason. Propose specific, actionable solutions that your country would realistically support. These must align with your delegation's foreign policy, economic capacity, and diplomatic relationships. A small island nation proposing a global carbon tax is less credible than proposing a regional adaptation fund. Your solutions should be concrete enough that another delegate could reference them when drafting a working paper.
✦ Your solutions must be realistic for YOUR country to propose. France can propose AI regulation frameworks because it hosts the Paris AI Action Summit. A smaller country might propose regional knowledge-sharing instead. Think about what your country can actually deliver.

Sample sentence: "France proposes the establishment of an international AI Safety Authority under UN coordination, building on the model of the International Atomic Energy Agency, to set global standards for high-risk AI systems."

6
Closing (10 seconds)
End your speech cleanly and professionally. Thank the chair by name or title, state that your delegation is ready to collaborate, and express willingness to work with all member states. Do not introduce new information here — the closing is about tone, not content. A strong closing leaves the committee with a sense of your professionalism and readiness to work.
💡 Keep your closing to one sentence. Thank the chair, state your delegation's readiness to collaborate, and stop. A clean ending is more memorable than a trailing one.

Sample sentence: "France looks forward to working with all delegations to build a safer, more ethical future for artificial intelligence. Thank you, Chair."

Speech Timeline Visual

A 90-second opening speech breaks down into these time allocations. Use this as a guide when rehearsing — time each section individually to make sure you are spending your minutes wisely.

Hook 15s
Issue 20s
Position 20s
Actions 15s
Solution 30s
Close 10s

The solution section takes the largest share of your speech because it is the part other delegates will reference when forming blocs and drafting working papers. The hook is short but disproportionately powerful — it determines whether anyone is listening for the rest. The country position and past actions sections prove you have done the work. The closing is brief but leaves a lasting impression of professionalism.

Full Sample Speech

Below is a real opening speech from Venezuela addressing the humanitarian immigration crisis in America. Each section is annotated so you can see how the six-part structure works in practice. Notice how the speech uses "the delegate" instead of "I" — a common MUN convention for maintaining formality.

Sample Opening Speech — Venezuela · Humanitarian Immigration Crisis in America
Opening · 0:00–0:10

"Honourable Chair, distinguished Delegates."

Step 1: Address the chair and fellow delegates. Formal, respectful, sets the tone.
Context · 0:10–0:30

"The delegation of Venezuela stands before this committee not just as a nation, but as the voice of millions forced to flee hardship, instability, and suffering in search of safety and dignity."

Step 2: Establishes emotional weight and immediately positions Venezuela as a stakeholder with moral authority on the issue.
Issue · 0:30–0:55

"The humanitarian immigration crisis in America is not simply about borders or politics — it is about human lives. Venezuelan families have crossed dangerous journeys to escape economic collapse, food shortages, political unrest, and insecurity. Many arrive in the United States seeking asylum and protection, only to face detention, deportation, and fear under strict immigration enforcement policies by agencies such as ICE. Recent reports show thousands of Venezuelans have faced deportation flights and the loss of humanitarian protections like TPS."

Step 3: Defines the problem with specific, concrete details — ICE, TPS, deportation flights. Not vague, not generic.
Position · 0:55–1:15

"Venezuela recognizes the right of every sovereign nation to protect its borders. However, the delegate strongly believes immigration policies must remain humane, lawful, and respectful of human rights."

Step 4: Acknowledges the counter-argument (sovereignty) before asserting Venezuela's position. This shows diplomatic maturity — the committee will respect a delegation that engages with the other side.
Solution · 1:15–1:35

"Therefore, Venezuela calls for stronger international cooperation, expanded humanitarian aid, fair asylum processes, and policies focused on compassion rather than punishment. The delegate urges nations to address the root causes of migration while protecting the dignity and rights of all migrants."

Step 5: Four concrete proposals — cooperation, aid, asylum processes, root causes. The "compassion rather than punishment" line is memorable and frames the debate.
Closing · 1:35–1:40

"Thank you."

Step 6: Short and clean. The speech speaks for itself — no need to over-explain at the end.

Delivery Tips

Writing a strong speech is only half the battle. How you deliver it determines whether the committee remembers it. These delivery practices separate average delegates from memorable ones.

✅ Do ❌ Don't
Make eye contact with the chair and scan the room Read directly from your paper without looking up
Speak slowly and clearly — about 130 words per minute Rush through points to cram in more content
Use formal diplomatic language throughout Use slang, filler words, or casual phrasing
Pause for 2 seconds after key points for emphasis Fill every silence with "um," "uh," or "so"
Stand or sit up straight with open posture Sway, fidget, or cross your arms defensively
Practice the speech out loud at least three times before committee Only read it silently in your head during preparation
Vary your tone to emphasize important words and phrases Speak in a monotone voice from start to finish
💡 Record yourself delivering the speech and play it back. You will catch pacing issues, awkward pauses, and unclear pronunciation that you miss in real time. Most phones have a voice memo app — use it.

Common Mistakes

Avoid these frequent errors that weaken opening speeches and cost delegates credibility with the chair and fellow delegates.

❌ Mistake ✅ Fix
Writing a position paper and reading it word-for-word Condense to 6 focused sections and speak from key bullet points
Spending 45 seconds on background and only 15 seconds on solutions Invert the ratio — spend the most time on what your country proposes
Saying "I think" or "In my opinion" Use "France believes" or "The delegation of France holds the position that"
Going over the time limit without noticing Use a stopwatch during practice and trim until you finish under 90 seconds
Introducing new topics or solutions in the closing line Use the closing only to thank the chair and signal readiness to collaborate
Apologizing for nervousness at the start ("Sorry if I'm shaking") Begin with your hook immediately — confidence comes from preparation
Mumbling the country name or committee name Enunciate clearly — these are the words delegates will use to identify you

Pre-Speech Checklist

Run through this checklist before committee begins to make sure your opening speech is ready to deliver.

  • ✓ Under 2 minutes when read aloud at a measured pace
  • ✓ Includes a hook in the first 10 seconds that grabs attention
  • ✓ States your country's position clearly and accurately
  • ✓ Proposes at least one specific, actionable solution
  • ✓ Addresses the chair formally by title
  • ✓ Practiced out loud at least twice before committee
  • ✓ Contains no personal opinions — only your delegation's official stance
  • ✓ References at least one specific UN resolution, treaty, or policy framework
  • ✓ Ends with a clean, professional closing — no trailing off
  • ✓ Timed and rehearsed with a stopwatch or timer

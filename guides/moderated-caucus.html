How to Speak in a Moderated Caucus

⚠️ MUN Pronoun Rule: In MUN, delegates represent countries — not themselves. Never use personal pronouns (I, we, us, me, my, our) in speeches, position papers, resolutions, or dialogue. Always use your country or delegation name instead. For example, say "The delegation of Japan supports…" rather than "I support…".

What is a Moderated Caucus?

A moderated caucus is the formal speaking portion of a Model UN committee session, controlled entirely by the chair. Unlike an unmoderated caucus where delegates move freely and negotiate in informal groups, a moderated caucus follows strict procedural rules: the chair calls on delegates one at a time, each speaker addresses the full committee from their seat, and speaking time is strictly enforced.

When a delegate or the chair motions for a moderated caucus, the committee enters a structured debate format. The chair opens a speakers' list and calls delegates in the order they raised their placards. Each delegate typically receives between 60 and 120 seconds — sometimes as little as 30 seconds in fast-paced committees — to deliver their remarks. If time expires mid-sentence, the chair will cut you off without exception.

Moderated caucuses serve three core purposes in committee. First, they allow delegates to formally present their country's position on the topic under discussion. Second, they create a forum for delegates to respond directly to points raised by other speakers, building on or challenging arguments in real time. Third, they give the chair and the committee a pulse check on where consensus is forming and where disagreements persist.

Unlike the opening speech — which is a one-time, prepared set piece — moderated caucus speeches happen repeatedly throughout a session. You might speak three, five, or even ten times in a single committee day. Each speech is an opportunity to advance your bloc's objectives, build alliances, and demonstrate to the chair that you are an engaged, well-prepared delegate. Mastering this format is one of the most important skills in Model UN, and it is the primary way delegates earn speaking awards.

Speech Structure

Every effective moderated caucus speech follows the same five-part framework. Whether you are delivering a 60-second reply or a two-minute position statement, this structure ensures your remarks are clear, respectful, and impactful.

1
Address the Chair
Always begin by addressing the chair directly. This is not optional — it is a procedural requirement in most conferences and a sign of formal diplomatic etiquette. Use the phrase "Honourable Chair," or "Mr./Madam Chair," depending on the chair's preference. Failing to address the chair before speaking is one of the most common rookie mistakes and immediately signals to the room that you are unfamiliar with UN procedure.
2
Acknowledge the Previous Speaker
Before making your own point, briefly acknowledge the delegate who spoke before you. This demonstrates active listening and builds credibility with the committee. Reference something specific they said — a claim, a proposal, or a statistic — and either agree with it, disagree with it, or build on it. Phrases like "The delegation of Japan thanks the delegate of France for their point on renewable energy funding, and the delegation of Japan would like to build on that idea" show the chair you are engaged in the debate, not just reading from a script.
💡 Never skip the acknowledgment. Even a single sentence like "The delegation of Japan thanks the delegate of India for their remarks" takes two seconds and makes a significant difference in how the chair perceives your professionalism.
3
State Your Main Point
This is the core of your speech. Deliver one clear, focused argument — not three, not five, just one. In a 60-second speech, you have time for a single well-developed point. State your claim, back it up with one piece of evidence (a statistic, a treaty reference, or a concrete example), and explain why it matters to the committee. Avoid vague statements like "this is an important issue." Instead, say something like "Cybersecurity breaches cost the global economy $8 trillion annually, and without a unified framework, this figure will double by 2030."
4
Propose or Support
End the substantive portion of your speech by either proposing a specific action or expressing support for an existing proposal. This gives the committee something concrete to work with and signals that you are solution-oriented, not just critical. If your bloc has drafted a working paper, mention it by name. If you are endorsing another delegate's idea, say so explicitly. The chair notices delegates who move the debate forward versus those who only state problems.
5
Close Formally
Always end with a formal closing. The standard phrase is "Thank you, Honourable Chair." Do not trail off, do not say "that's it," and do not add informal filler like "so yeah." A crisp, formal close leaves a professional impression and signals to the chair that you have finished speaking and they can move to the next delegate. Some delegates also add "I yield my time back to the chair" if they finish early, which is a nice touch that shows procedural awareness.

Types of Moderated Caucus Speeches

Not all moderated caucus speeches serve the same purpose. Understanding the three main types — and when to use each — will help you speak more strategically throughout the session.

R
Reply Speech
A reply speech responds directly to a point made by another delegate. This is the most common type of moderated caucus speech because committees are dynamic — delegates are constantly making claims, proposing ideas, and challenging each other. A strong reply speech agrees or disagrees with a specific point, provides a counterargument or supporting evidence, and ties the response back to your country's position. The key is to be specific: reference the delegate by country name and paraphrase their exact claim before responding. This shows the committee you were listening and adds intellectual rigor to the debate.

Sample script: "Honourable Chair, the delegation of Japan thanks the delegate of Germany for raising the issue of cross-border data regulation. However, the delegation of Japan would like to point out that a one-size-fits-all regulatory framework fails to account for the differing technological capacities of developing nations. Japan instead proposes a tiered compliance system that scales with a country's digital infrastructure, ensuring that regulation protects citizens without stifling innovation."

P
Position Speech
A position speech formally states your country's stance on the topic. This is typically delivered early in the session, during the first or second moderated caucus, when the committee is still establishing where each delegation stands. A position speech introduces your country's policy, references relevant treaties or agreements your country has signed, and signals which bloc or coalition your delegation is aligned with. It is more structured than a reply speech and often draws directly from your position paper research.

Sample script: "Honourable Chair, the delegation of Japan firmly believes that cybersecurity is a matter of national security and economic stability. Japan has invested over $10 billion in its National Cybersecurity Strategy and is a signatory to the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime. The delegation of Japan supports the creation of an international CERT coordination framework and urges all member states to adopt baseline cyber hygiene standards."

M
Motion Speech
A motion speech proposes a specific topic for the committee to discuss during a moderated caucus. When you motion for a moderated caucus, you suggest a focused sub-topic — for example, "a moderated caucus on the topic of climate finance mechanisms for developing nations." The chair will typically ask you to briefly explain why this topic deserves discussion time. A strong motion speech is brief, frames the topic clearly, and explains why it is relevant to the broader debate. This is a procedural move, but it demonstrates leadership and strategic thinking.

Sample script: "Honourable Chair, the delegation of Canada motions for a moderated caucus on the topic of public-private partnerships in cybersecurity infrastructure. As the committee has spent significant time discussing state-level regulation, it is critical that the committee also address the role of technology companies in building resilient systems. This topic will allow delegates to explore concrete collaboration models."

Full Sample Speeches

Below are two complete moderated caucus speeches — one reply speech and one position speech — annotated so you can see how the five-part structure works in practice.

Sample 1 — Reply Speech (Climate Finance)

"Honourable Chair,

Step 1: Address the chair. Formal, respectful, procedural.

The delegation of Japan thanks the delegate of Brazil for their remarks on the urgency of climate adaptation funding. The delegation of Japan agrees that current financial commitments fall far short of what developing nations need.

Step 2: Acknowledge the previous speaker. Specific reference to their point.

However, the delegation of Japan would like to note that the Green Climate Fund has disbursed only $34 billion of its $100 billion annual target as of 2025, and the gap is growing. The issue is not a lack of pledges — it is a lack of disbursement mechanisms that reach communities directly. Japan's experience with the Adaptation Fund shows that channeling resources through national implementing entities, rather than multilateral intermediaries, reduces delivery time by 40% and ensures funds reach the projects that need them most.

Step 3: Main point. One clear argument with a specific statistic.

Japan therefore supports the establishment of a streamlined climate finance disbursement mechanism under UNDP coordination, and the delegation of Japan urges all member states to consider this model in the working paper currently under discussion.

Step 4: Propose or support. Concrete action tied to an existing proposal.

Thank you, Honourable Chair."

Step 5: Formal close.
Sample 2 — Position Speech (Cybersecurity)

"Honourable Chair,

Step 1: Address the chair.

The delegation of Japan thanks the delegate of the United Kingdom for raising the critical issue of state-sponsored cyberattacks. The delegation of Japan recognizes this as one of the defining security challenges of our time.

Step 2: Acknowledge the previous speaker and transition to your position.

Japan's position is clear: cybersecurity must be treated as a collective security issue, not a domestic policy matter. In 2024 alone, Japan recorded over 17,000 significant cyber incidents targeting critical infrastructure, including hospitals, power grids, and financial institutions. The economic cost exceeded ¥400 billion. No single nation can defend against these threats alone. Japan is a signatory to the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime and has bilateral cybersecurity cooperation agreements with 28 nations. The delegation of Japan firmly believes that an international CERT coordination framework — modeled on the existing FIRST (Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams) network — would provide the real-time intelligence sharing that the current threat landscape demands.

Step 3: Main point. Country-specific data and a concrete proposal.

Japan supports the draft resolution's call for a UN-affiliated CERT coordination body and urges this committee to include mandatory reporting protocols for state-sponsored attacks, which are currently absent from international cyber norms.

Step 4: Support a specific proposal in the working paper.

Thank you, Honourable Chair."

Step 5: Formal close.

Time Management

In a 60-second moderated caucus speech, every second counts. Below is the optimal time allocation for a one-minute speech. Adjust proportionally if your committee allows 90 or 120 seconds.

Chair5 sec
Acknowledge10 sec
Main Point40 sec
Proposal30 sec
Close5 sec
💡 Practice your speech with a stopwatch before the session. Most delegates underestimate how fast 60 seconds passes. If your speech takes longer than 55 seconds in practice, cut it down — you do not want the chair cutting you off mid-sentence.

Key timing tips:

  • Keep your chair address and acknowledgment under 15 seconds combined. These are necessary but not where your value lies.
  • Spend the majority of your time — 40 to 50 seconds — on your main point and supporting evidence. This is what the chair and other delegates will remember.
  • If you are proposing something, allocate 20 to 30 seconds. Be specific enough that another delegate could reference your proposal in their own speech.
  • Never use filler words like "um," "so," or "like." These eat into your time and undermine your credibility. Practice until your delivery is clean.
  • If you finish early, say "I yield my remaining time to the chair" rather than padding your speech with repetition. This looks confident and procedurally aware.

Common Mistakes

Avoid these frequent errors that weaken moderated caucus speeches and cost delegates points with chairs.

❌ Don't ✅ Do
Skip addressing the chair and jump straight into your argument Always open with "Honourable Chair," — it takes one second and is procedurally required
Try to make three or four points in a 60-second speech Make one clear, well-supported point per speech — quality over quantity
Say "The delegation of Japan agrees with the previous speaker" without explaining what specifically the delegation agrees with Reference their exact claim: "The delegation of Japan thanks the delegate of X for their point on Y, and the delegation of Japan would add…"
Read your speech word-for-word from a piece of paper without looking up Use bullet-point notes and make eye contact with the committee — it conveys confidence
Use filler words ("um," "so," "like," "basically") that eat into your time Practice your delivery until it is clean — silence between sentences is perfectly fine
Let the chair cut you off mid-sentence because you ran over time Practice with a stopwatch and aim to finish at 55 seconds, leaving a 5-second buffer
Speak only once in the entire session and expect to win an award Speak frequently — aim for at least 3 to 5 moderated caucus speeches per session
Make personal attacks or dismiss another delegate's country Challenge ideas, not people: "The delegation of Japan respectfully disagrees with the proposed framework because…"

Pre-Speech Checklist

Run through this checklist before every moderated caucus speech to make sure you are prepared and professional.

  • ☐ Addressed the chair at the start of your speech
  • ☐ Stayed within the time limit (practiced with a stopwatch)
  • ☐ Made a single, clear, focused point (not three scattered ideas)
  • ☐ Referenced at least one earlier speech by another delegate
  • ☐ Included a specific piece of evidence (statistic, treaty, or resolution)
  • ☐ Proposed or supported a concrete action or working paper
  • ☐ Closed formally with "Thank you, Honourable Chair"
  • ☐ Avoided filler words and maintained eye contact with the room
